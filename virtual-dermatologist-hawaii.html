Skin care in Hawaii

The same American Cancer Society analysis in the International Journal of Cancer that ranked states by ultraviolet-attributable melanoma placed Hawaiʻi at the top: 65.1 cases per 100,000 among non-Hispanic white residents, against a national low of 15.1 in Alaska. The study also estimated that 97.3% of melanomas diagnosed in Hawaiʻi were attributable to ultraviolet exposure — the highest attributable proportion of any state.

Latitude explains part of it. Hawaiʻi sits closer to the equator than any other state, the ultraviolet index stays high year-round rather than falling away in winter, and water reflects a meaningful additional dose onto skin already receiving it directly. There is no seasonal break during which accumulated exposure pauses.

Geography compounds the access problem in a way it does not on the mainland. Specialist dermatology is concentrated on Oʻahu, and residents of Maui, Kauaʻi, the Big Island, Molokaʻi, and Lānaʻi may face an inter-island flight for an appointment that consists of a conversation and a prescription. For routine treatment and follow-up, that is a considerable amount of travel for a small amount of care.

Practical note on sunscreen: Hawaiʻi restricts the sale of sunscreens containing oxybenzone and octinoxate. Mineral formulations based on zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are widely available, effective, and generally better tolerated by sensitive and acne-prone skin in any case.

Frequently asked questions

Is Dr. Porto licensed in Hawaii?

Yes. Dr. Porto holds an active medical license in Hawaii and in nine other states. He is double board-certified in dermatology and in Mohs micrographic surgery, and is a Fellow of the American Academy of Dermatology.

Do I need to live in Hawaii to be seen?

What matters is where you are physically located during the consultation, not where you permanently reside. Physicians are licensed state by state, so being in Hawaii at the time of your consult is what allows Dr. Porto to treat you there.

How long does it take to be seen?

Far less time than the national norm for dermatology. Survey data has put the average wait for a dermatology appointment in the United States at around five weeks, with wide variation between states. Sending a message does not require waiting for a scheduled appointment slot at all.

What happens if my condition needs a procedure?

You will be told directly, and pointed toward what the next step should be. Dr. Porto is a Mohs surgeon as well as a general dermatologist, so the assessment of whether something needs surgical or procedural treatment comes from someone who performs it.

Which sunscreens can I use in Hawaiʻi?

Hawaiʻi restricts the sale of sunscreens containing oxybenzone and octinoxate. Mineral sunscreens using zinc oxide or titanium dioxide comply, protect across the ultraviolet spectrum, and tend to suit sensitive and acne-prone skin well.
